The city improved one step for applicants: submission fell from 40 to 10 minutes. The unresolved problem is the time and uncertainty between requesting permission and receiving a decision that allows urgent work to proceed. Approval still takes 30 days, and the shared engineering, land-record and inspection services constrain that flow. A chatbot may help people submit complete information, but the facts do not establish that questions at intake are what keeps cases waiting.

Use an end-to-end service boundary from an applicant's first attempt to apply through a communicated approval, refusal or other explicit disposition. Track applicant work and resubmission as well as the city's work. Engineering review, land records and any inspections needed before a decision belong within the analysis even when another organization controls them. Distinguish that analysis boundary from the director's authority to change staffing, priorities or outside commitments. Track permission to start urgent work as an outcome; construction execution and later inspections can remain outside the initial improvement scope unless they actually gate that permission.

| Measure | What it resolves |
| --- | --- |
| Calendar time from initial submission to decision, with median, upper-tail and oldest unresolved age, separated by urgent/routine and case complexity | Whether service is improving for applicants, including people still waiting. Confirm what the reported 30 days means. |
| Time at each handoff: ready and waiting, active review, blocked for information, and awaiting an outside response | Where delay accumulates and who can change it; account for parallel activities without adding overlapping times twice. |
| Arrival and completion rates, work in progress and backlog age by stage and priority | Whether queues are growing and whether moving work merely shifts the delay. |
| First-submission completeness, rework and repeated contacts | Whether intake assistance, including a chatbot, addresses a material cause of delay. |
| Urgent cases meeting an agreed need-by date and reasons for misses | Whether the service supports time-critical work; define urgency and escalation ownership explicitly. |
| Decision accuracy, required-review completion, avoidable reversals and applicant access across channels | Whether faster processing preserves sound decisions and access to the service. |

The next decision should authorize a bounded examination of actual cases and a small flow-improvement pilot, with a named service owner and participation from the three shared services. Reconstruct event timelines for recent routine, urgent and still-open cases; establish whether each bottleneck is waiting for scarce capacity, missing information, scheduling or a dependency. Agree baseline measures, pilot scope and review date before changing the process. No numeric improvement target is justified yet.

If complete cases spend most of their time waiting for engineering or inspections, pilot coordinated scheduling, a visible shared queue and an explicit urgent-case route with agreed capacity. Preserve required checks, monitor effects on routine cases and prevent indefinite starvation. If records requests or incomplete submissions dominate, first address access, information exchange or application guidance. A chatbot merits a bounded trial only if evidence shows that the questions it can answer contribute materially to rework or delay; compare its impact on complete submissions and total decision time, including the human support it requires.

Three facts would most sharpen the decision: where urgent cases spend their 30 days; who can approve urgency and allocate shared capacity; and whether engineering, records and inspections can proceed concurrently. The conditional recommendation is to improve the limiting stage and its handoffs first, then decide whether a chatbot is a useful part of that change.
